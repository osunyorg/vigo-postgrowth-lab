At the same time, public participation is increasingly regarded as an opportunity to horizontalize governance, while acknowledging its potential to reproduce the system. However, limited attention has been paid to how power relations shape participatory processes and their contributions to transitions, and how participation may, in turn, transform or reproduce existing power relations. Against this background, this dissertation examines the relationship between power and public participation in sustainability transitions through two case studies: a multi-stakeholder engagement process and a co-creative citizen science initiative. By analysing how power relations shape participatory processes and how participation may, in turn, influence existing power configurations, the thesis explores the conditions under which public participation can contribute to or hinder societal change, as well as how power relations shape sustainability transition pathways. The thesis contributes to STR by advancing an understanding of this reciprocal relationship, while providing insights for policymakers and practitioners on how participatory processes can be designed to address different contexts, challenges, and democratic objectives.    13.30 Victor José Sánchez Juárez  "Just" for Whom? The Politics of Just Transition in Spain: Trade Unions, the State and Social Movements

Just Transition seems to be in every transnational negotiation concerning the eco-social transition. It started as a demand from the labour movement and is now part of ILO guidelines, the Paris Agreement and Spain's own climate policy. Governments, trade unions and social movements all use it, but they rarely mean the same thing. Just Transition scholarship has mostly asked what a just transition should involve, often assuming that the State will manage it and that social dialogue is the right way to govern it. However, less attention has been paid to how the meaning of Just Transition is itself fought over, and to what this struggle includes or leaves out.Against this background, this dissertation treats Just Transition as a (counter)hegemonic project. It combines Laclau and Mouffe's discourse theory, the Logics Approach and frame analysis to study how Spanish trade unions, the state and social movements define, hold in place and challenge its meaning. It draws on policy and organisational documents and on semi-structured interviews, and looks at two cases: the closure of the coal industry and the conflict over the Touro copper mine in Galicia. The thesis contributes to Just Transition research by explaining how its political edge was turned into consensus and where it comes back. It also contributes to discourse theory by showing step by step how logics can be applied to empirical material. Cutting, pruning and (un)mashing     "If you want to make God laugh, tell him your plans."This presentation revisits the development of my PhD research over the past year, focusing on how the research has taken shape through decisions, adjustments and clarifications. The thesis examines how peasants in Mexico have historically navigated between imposed and alienated technologies, on the one hand, and appropriated and appropriate technologies, on the other. Here, I trace how I have progressively worked towards addressing this issue by refining the conceptual framework, methodological approach and comparative case study design.I begin by briefly situating the research within the broader context of agriculture and technology, considering the enduring presence of peasant agriculture and its relationship with technological change. I then revisit the conceptual framework, discussing key clarifications and decisions made since last year's presentation, particularly concerning the definition of technology and the conceptual boundaries of the study.Next, I address the evolution of the methodological approach. The initial proposal sought to combine critical discourse analysis, ethnoecology and technography to examine three interconnected dimensions of peasant-technology relations: power, the mediation of human-environment interactions, and the material practices of technological production and use. Underlying this methodological mashing was the assumption that combining multiple approaches would yield a more comprehensive analytical framework. However, this stacking produced an unnecessarily convoluted framework that proved cumbersome in the field. These difficulties compelled me to make a research decision despite my unease about doing so, and to commit to one approach: a politically and ecologically grounded technography. This approach proved far more operational and fruitful for examining technology as a situated practice, moving beyond technological artefacts to explore processes of making, the distribution and coordination of knowledge and skills, and the rules that organise technological practices.The presentation then turns to the comparative case study design. While the initial proposal centred on comparing an eco-technologies network in Michoacán with Irrigation District 018 in Sonora, fieldwork revealed a mismatch in scale and analytical units. This led to a redefinition of the comparison around peasant agricultural technology in the Pátzcuaro watershed in Michoacán and the Yaqui watershed in Sonora. Alongside the conceptual and methodological pruning, these adjustments provided the basis for developing an ever-changing abductive coding strategy, which with the upcoming phase of fieldwork in Sonora will inform the cross-case comparison.Overall, this presentation is not about presenting a finished product, but about walking through the process of how the project has taken shape. It reflects on the non-linear movement between planning, fieldwork and analysis. In doing so, working on the thesis has also meant thinking about how I think about the research: testing early assumptions, recognising their limits and refining the choices along the way.

Aitor Alonso Rodríguez

Power and Public Participation in Sustainability Transitions: Collective Practices and Decision-Making

Sustainability Transitions Research (STR) has increasingly recognised power as a key concept for understanding the social and political dynamics underlying sustainability transitions. At the same time, public participation is increasingly regarded as an opportunity to horizontalize governance, while acknowledging its potential to reproduce the system. However, limited attention has been paid to how power relations shape participatory processes and their contributions to transitions, and how participation may, in turn, transform or reproduce existing power relations. Against this background, this dissertation examines the relationship between power and public participation in sustainability transitions through two case studies: a multi-stakeholder engagement process and a co-creative citizen science initiative. By analysing how power relations shape participatory processes and how participation may, in turn, influence existing power configurations, the thesis explores the conditions under which public participation can contribute to or hinder societal change, as well as how power relations shape sustainability transition pathways. The thesis contributes to STR by advancing an understanding of this reciprocal relationship, while providing insights for policymakers and practitioners on how participatory processes can be designed to address different contexts, challenges, and democratic objectives.

 "Just" for Whom? The Politics of Just Transition in Spain: Trade Unions, the State and Social Movements

Just Transition seems to be in every transnational negotiation concerning the eco-social transition. It started as a demand from the labour movement and is now part of ILO guidelines, the Paris Agreement and Spain's own climate policy. Governments, trade unions and social movements all use it, but they rarely mean the same thing. Just Transition scholarship has mostly asked what a just transition should involve, often assuming that the State will manage it and that social dialogue is the right way to govern it. However, less attention has been paid to how the meaning of Just Transition is itself fought over, and to what this struggle includes or leaves out.Against this background, this dissertation treats Just Transition as a (counter)hegemonic project. The thesis contributes to Just Transition research by explaining how its political edge was turned into consensus and where it comes back. It also contributes to discourse theory by showing step by step how logics can be applied to empirical material. During the presentation, I will share preliminary findings.
